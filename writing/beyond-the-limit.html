Beyond the Limit

first published 2023 · moved here

Essays Poems

What do condiment squeeze bottles and racing cars driving have in common? Besides both being found at the circuit on a fall weekend there is something about these two tools that can be easily overlooked. In the hands of an expert there is a process taking place invisible to the observer, though the most keen among them may know of it. The process is the difference between a messy plate, and an Instagram worthy hot-dog. If you were to ask Max Verstappen how he is able to drive his car so fast, he would tell you he has a good "feel" for the car. Dale Earnhardt probably would have said the same about his car. Martin Armstrong, as a trader, said this of his ticker tape machine. In a meeting with Margaret Thatcher, he quoted her as using the same expression with respect to the election following her retirement. Something about Time...

The circuit for me was just a tunnel, which I was going going going, and I realised I was far beyond my conscious understanding.

Ayrton Senna

Why do we practice? What is it really about repeating something over and over again, failing endless towards a goal, that separates the professionals from everybody else? Activities we perform, whether physical or otherwise, are stratified across some spectrum of skill and ability. Watch someone chop wood for 10 minutes, and you'd probably have an idea of how much time they've spent chopping wood. The same could be said of a game of League of Legends, or a lap around the track. Repeating an action is what allows us to improve our process, to improve at performing that action. A great deal of time must be spent practising to reap rewards, and not only that. To practice, conscious effort must be applied towards this repetition, an examination of your own flaws towards the ends of slowly reducing their effect on your process.

Passage of time is not so much our choice, but as it passes there is an effect of how much attention we are paying to what we are doing. So we don't spend time, but we spend our attention. The one thing we humans may, or may not, have a firm grip on at any given moment. Attention is a tool that has to be honed, to encompass all our senses and the data they gather for us. This is the information that drives all our unconscious processes, yet much to our chagrin remains subject to our conscious control. Practice is the process of the body collecting information, creating associations and muscle instincts as a product. As if our body has its own circuit with memory, capable of transmitting information at a rate exponentially faster than our brains can process it. Because what top-tier performer has time to think?

Getting into the flow is just as, maybe even more important than practice. Described by a variety of authors in a variety of ways, the flow is a state we enter in performing an activity, when our attention is completely devoted. A state where we lose ourselves, lose our sense of time, and on the best of occasions perform a miracle on ice in front of cheering crowds. It is the most absolute application of our attention, the full potential of the human nervous system at work. When all that we have to give to time is focused on one single activity, having been repeated so many times no mind need be paid to the motions. Just the senses, and the data.

Nothing is perfect. This is news to nobody, living on this big crazy planet. Yet somewhere in the theory, to every problem there must be an exact solution. It may never be found, or may not even be possible, but it will always exist. However, you can get very very close to it. Rubber will never have perfect traction with the road, but a great race-car driver can put it as close to this limit as may be physically possible. The absolute greatest force that can be applied, without losing the perfect line. There is some threshold of force applied that will make the mustard come out of the bottle, a little practice may reveal where it is. Just like that, some abstract mathematical function is solved by a bag of flesh and bones. A perfect synthesis of man and machine. Mercedes-AMG F1 team has computer models which make projections about the fastest possible lap, used as reference for the driver to extract maximum performance. Since modern Formula One is driven by data. A system perhaps more well refined, perfected, than the computer, Lewis Hamilton has beaten these projections on several occasions. He can be found on the limit of time and space, where consciousness hides its richest treasures.

"Stilgar," Alia said, fighting to hold him, "you stand in a valley between dunes. I stand on the crest. I see where you do not see. And, among other things, I see mountains which conceal the distances."

Frank Herbert, Dune Messiah
